A decade ago, I read about 200 words per minute, like most readers. Then I discovered speed reading skills, which have helped me double and triple my pace without sacrificing comprehension. So with proper training, you can also read faster than ever before.

One, minimize subvocalization.

Many readers say words in their heads while reading. This is called subvocalization. Studies show that it actually prevents you from reading faster. Normally, our talking speed is about 200 words per minute.

If you subvocalize while reading, you are never going to reach your reading potential. You do not need to speak every word in your head in order to understand what you read. Try to minimize subvocalization. Go faster than you normally read, so that your brain doesn't have the chance to have the inner monologue. Listening to instrumental music while reading also helps stop subvocalization.

Two, reduce fixations.

Your eyes do not read in a straight line, but jump and stop on words. Fixation happens when it stops on words. A study shows that more experienced readers make fewer fixations and have shorter fixation durations.

You need to reduce the number of fixations by training your peripheral vision. Your vision can span about 1.5 inches at a time, which covers four or five words on the page. Don't focus on one word at a time. Try to spread your gaze.

Make sure your eyes take in a chunk of words at a time. Let's say you are reading The Old Man and the Sea. You can read "the old man and the big fish" all at once.

Three, eliminate regression.

Don't reread the text you already read. This prevents you from reading faster. This interferes with your understanding of the material. Researchers found that good readers don't make regressions.

Here's the easiest way to break this bad habit. Use a pen or your finger. Let your eyes follow it. Don't stop or go back. Since you know there's no way to go back, you are more likely to improve your concentration when reading.

Four, read extensively.

I remember that in my first year of college, I could only process 200 words per minute. When I graduated from college, my reading speed tripled. How did I achieve that? Simply by reading a lot.

I must give credit to my professors at Wenzhou-Kean University, a China-US cooperative university. They required me to read a few hundred pages of books and reading materials for different courses on a weekly basis. I had to write a few papers every week, do presentations every week, and take tests every month. All these assignments were based on extensive reading.

I was overwhelmed by the workload at first, but the more I read, the stronger foundation of knowledge I had. And then I became a better reader and communicator.

When studying in an American graduate school, reading American journals, and working at news organizations, I felt really comfortable reading stacks of research papers, books and newspapers on a daily basis. So reading extensively really benefited my learning a lot.
